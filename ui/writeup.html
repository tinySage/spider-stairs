Teaching an eight-legged robot to climb stairs.

12 cm steps · 78% of its standing height Real replay · trained policy · no exploration noise

Eight legs, 24 motors, and a staircase it has never seen. Nobody told it how to walk. This is what it worked out, and the two ways it cheated first.

The taskA spider is dropped onto a tile of stairs and given one instruction: walk that way. Everything between the instruction and the motion is learned.

Walking is easy. Stairs are not.

Flat-ground walking is close to a solved problem in simulation. A policy finds a rhythm, the rhythm holds, and nothing in the world argues with it. Stairs break that. The ground stops being a plane and becomes a sequence of decisions: this foot clears the riser, that one does not, and the body has to pitch to let the back legs follow.

Eight legs make the problem both easier and harder. Easier because the spider is statically stable almost all the time, so a bad step rarely ends the episode. Harder because there are 24 joints to coordinate instead of 12, eight contact points arguing with each other, and a much larger space of gaits that technically work but look nothing like walking.

The tallest step here is 12 cm against a standing height of 15.4 cm. For a person of average height, that is a staircase with steps a little over a metre tall.

The animalGenerated from a parameter spec rather than modelled by hand, so leg count, segment lengths and masses are all variables.

Building the spider

The body is an ellipsoid with an abdomen behind it, and each of the eight legs is a three-link chain: a hip that swings the leg fore and aft, a hip that lifts it, and a knee that folds the shin. The resting pose holds the knees above the body, which is the detail that makes stair climbing possible at all. A leg with its knee below the body can only push; a leg with its knee above can reach.

The policy does not command torques directly. It outputs 24 joint angles fifty times a second, and a position controller running at 200 Hz chases them. That split matters: the policy works in a space where a sensible output looks like a pose, not like a set of forces, and the controller absorbs the physics in between.

The terrainSteps are square rings around a central platform, so a spider walking out in any direction meets a staircase, and diagonal headings cross the corners.

A staircase that cannot be dodged

Each training tile is six metres square with a flat platform at its centre. Half the tiles are pits: the spider starts at the bottom and has to climb out. The other half are pyramids, where it starts on the summit and has to come down. Descending turns out to be a genuinely different skill, and the policy learns it first.

At the start of every episode the spider gets a random heading and a random speed between 0.25 and 0.6 m/s. Because the steps are concentric, there is no direction that avoids them, and no gait that works only when the stairs are straight ahead.

The curriculumDifficulty is not scheduled by iteration count. It follows what each spider can actually do.

Let the stairs grow

Dropping a fresh policy onto 12 cm steps teaches it nothing except how to fall over. So the tiles are arranged into levels, from 2 cm steps up to 12 cm, and every spider keeps its own position in that ladder. Finish a staircase and it moves up a level. Fail to get halfway and it moves down. Spiders that clear the hardest level are recycled to a random one, so the population never abandons the easy stairs entirely.

The useful property is that difficulty tracks ability per spider rather than per run. At any moment the population is spread across the ladder, and the average level is a far better progress signal than reward, which actually falls as the stairs get harder.

Two ways it cheated

Reward functions for legged robots are usually inherited rather than derived, and the standard set is tuned for quadrupeds that run at a metre per second. This spider walks at about a third of that, and the mismatch produced two failures that a training curve cannot show you.

It learned to stand still

Velocity tracking is normally rewarded with a Gaussian kernel around the commanded speed. With the standard width, a spider commanded to walk at 0.3 m/s and standing perfectly motionless still collected between 53 and 78 per cent of the available reward. Walking is expensive and risky; standing is free. The policy found that out in a few hundred iterations.

Fix. A kernel five times tighter, plus a dense term that pays for the fraction of commanded speed actually achieved, so that standing still scores zero rather than most of the points.

It learned to vibrate

The second policy reported 81 per cent of episodes finishing their staircase. Switch the exploration noise off, as you would at deployment, and the spider moved at 0.01 m/s. It had not learned to walk. It had learned that random jitter applied to 24 joints will ratchet a light eight-legged body along on friction, and the noise injected during training was doing the walking for it. The policy's own contribution was to hold a pose that made the jitter productive.

Fix. A penalty on any foot that slides while it is in contact with the ground. Ratcheting requires planted feet to slip, so pricing that behaviour removed the exploit, and a real alternating gait appeared in its place.

The lesson is cheap to state and expensive to learn: evaluate the deterministic policy, not the training statistics. A number that goes up is not evidence of the behaviour you wanted.

The run256 spiders, 500 iterations, about 3 million simulated steps, on two CPU cores. Roughly one per cent of the planned training budget.

Where it stands

The results below come from the small demo run, which exists to prove the pipeline rather than to solve the task. During training, with exploration noise on, 83 per cent of episodes finished their staircase. Evaluated properly, one attempt per staircase with the noise off, the picture is more modest.

Three of the four descents finished, including the tallest stairs. Not one ascent did. That split is not surprising: going down, gravity helps and the spider mostly has to avoid falling, while going up it has to lift its whole mass over a riser nearly as tall as it stands, eight times per step. The odd result is the 5.5 cm descent, where the spider sat on the summit and never committed to the first step down, which is the kind of local optimum more training usually clears.

What is left

The immediate step is the full training run: 4096 spiders instead of 256, 3000 iterations instead of 500. The demo saturates long before the policy has seen enough of the hard stairs to solve them, and the curriculum data suggests the population was still climbing when it stopped.

After that, two changes worth trying. A foot-clearance reward tied to the height of the next riser, which would attack the ascent failure directly rather than hoping the policy discovers clearance on its own. And removing the terrain scan from the policy's observations, so that it has to feel the stairs through joint feedback instead of being handed a height map. The second is the harder and more interesting problem, because it is the one a real robot faces.
